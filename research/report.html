Landing page research: best practices & competitors

Prepared October 2026 for the Treehouse co-founders. Based on the Treehouse poster plus web research.

How to read this. Each finding has a label. Research means a study with a stated method. Standard means an official rule or guideline (W3C, Google). Opinion means an expert's view or my judgement. The research environment blocked direct visits to most websites, so competitor details and some quotes come from search-result excerpts. Please open the links to confirm before quoting them publicly.

Summary: top 5 recommendations

  1. Lead with your niche, in the first screen. "Design & AI for nonprofits and AI-for-good teams" is rare in Toronto. Buyers pick specialists over generalists.
  2. Say what you don't do. "We don't build your product" is your clearest difference from Toronto agencies like Akendi and Bridgeable.
  3. Show starting prices. Usability research found business buyers get frustrated and leave when prices are hidden. Most consultancies hide them, so this also sets you apart.
  4. Make the 1-day workshop the easy first step. A small, fixed-price offer lowers the risk for a nonprofit with a tight budget.
  5. Make up for staying anonymous. Trust comes from real people. Without names, give specific experience, one clear contact path, and a plain reason why names come on the call.

Best practices for a consulting firm website

ResearchMost clients come from referrals. The website confirms you're credible.

"the most common way buyers find a new firm is by asking another person"Hinge Research Institute, survey of 822 buyers and 533 firms. Read the study. Caveat: published 2013, and Hinge sells marketing services.

The same study found expertise and specialized skills were the most important factors when buyers chose a firm. A later Hinge study says over 80% of buyers check a firm's website (Finding Experts; figure from a search excerpt, please verify).

For Treehouse: someone sends a link to a nonprofit director. In 10 seconds the page must say who you help and what you do. Name the niche in the hero and the "Now accepting" band.

ResearchShow prices, or at least starting prices.

Business customers "report pricing as the top most needed piece of information online," and people see firms that show it "as being genuine and forthright."Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g), usability studies with business users. Pricing information gives B2B sites a competitive advantage. Quoted from a search excerpt; please verify wording.

For Treehouse: put "From $X" on the workshop, assessment and monthly partnership. The landing page has placeholders ready.

ResearchPeople trust organizations that show real people behind them.

"Show that honest and trustworthy people stand behind your site."Stanford Web Credibility Project, guideline 4, based on research with over 4,500 people. Read the guidelines.

NN/g's "About Us" research (100 sites tested, 65 more reviewed) found users look at company information to decide whether to trust an organization, and are skeptical of stock photos and corporate jargon (NN/g article).

For Treehouse: staying anonymous has a real trust cost. The page offsets it with specific experience (sectors, years, team change), a plain "why no names" note, and a promise that names and LinkedIn profiles come on the call.

ResearchWrite headings that people can scan.

NN/g eye-tracking found people often read only headings and subheadings (the "layer-cake" pattern) and skip body text unless a heading matches what they want (NN/g layer-cake pattern).

For Treehouse: headings say what each section is ("Three problems we hear most", "Frequently asked questions"), not clever slogans.

StandardMake it accessible and fast.

Aim for WCAG 2.2 level AA: text contrast at least 4.5:1, tap targets at least 24×24 pixels, keyboard access and visible focus (W3C WCAG 2.2). Aim for Google's "good" Core Web Vitals: main content loads within 2.5 seconds (web.dev). This matters even more for nonprofits, who often serve the public and have accessibility duties under Ontario's AODA.

StandardGoogle and AI search need nothing special.

"There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode"Google Search Central. AI features and your website.

For Treehouse: keep all content as real text, give the page a clear title and description, and add basic structured data. The landing page does all three. Be wary of paid "AI SEO" services; their claims are unproven.

OpinionOne call to action, repeated with the same words.

Every button says "Book a free intro call". Expert consensus, not a study: one clear next step reduces hesitation. An empty blog or a "Resources" page with nothing in it looks worse than none, so add one only when you'll keep writing.

How Treehouse can stand out

  1. Niche plus offer. Plenty of firms do AI training. Plenty serve nonprofits. None I found help nonprofit design teams adopt AI and keep it working. Say this plainly. Research backs specialization (Hinge).
  2. "We leave your team stronger." Against agencies, your promise is capability, not deliverables. The comparison table on the landing page makes this concrete. Opinion
  3. Transparent starting prices. Few consultancies show them. Research says buyers want them (NN/g).
  4. A low-risk first step. A fixed 1-day workshop is easier to approve than a multi-month contract, especially on a nonprofit budget. Opinion
  5. Responsible AI as a feature. Nonprofits handle sensitive data and public trust. Your "AI operations that last" service (guardrails, privacy and bias checks) speaks to that worry directly. Opinion
  6. Proof as soon as you have it. One anonymized case study ("a Toronto health nonprofit with a 6-person design team") with a real result will do more than any other change. Only publish with the client's permission.

Your names and your day jobs

This is general information, not legal advice. Book a one-hour call with an Ontario employment lawyer before your first paid client.

What the law says

  • Canadian law doesn't ban side work. It becomes a problem when it breaks your contract or code of conduct, or creates a conflict of interest, for example working for your employer's competitor or for someone your employer does business with (Bennett Jones, L&E Global).
  • If your employer's policy requires you to disclose conflicts and you don't, the employer may be able to fire you for cause, without severance.
  • In Atkins v. Windsor Star, an Ontario court sided with an employee because the employer had no clear moonlighting policy: It was incumbent upon the employer to spell out, in language easily understood, exactly what its views and expectations were (HR Reporter). So your own written policies matter a lot.
  • Ontario banned most employee non-compete clauses in 2021, but confidentiality, non-solicitation and intellectual property clauses still apply (McMillan LLP). Executives can still be bound by non-competes.

Checklist for each co-founder

  • Read your employment contract, code of conduct and any outside-work or conflict-of-interest policy.
  • Don't take clients who compete with, buy from, or sell to your employer.
  • Never use your employer's time, laptop, accounts, templates or confidential information for Treehouse.
  • Check who owns what you create. Some contracts claim ideas made outside work hours.
  • If your policy asks for disclosure, disclose in writing and get written approval. That is also the path to putting your names on the site.
  • Use Treehouse-only accounts: a domain email, a booking link and a LinkedIn company page. Don't link personal profiles yet.

What the landing page does about it

Founders appear as "two co-founders" with real experience but no names or photos. A short note explains that names and LinkedIn profiles are shared on the intro call. When you're cleared, swap the experience card for two named bios with photos; trust research says that will help.
